1Why Spencer, and What Changes

Bishop Simplified satisfies moment equilibrium about the circle centre and vertical force equilibrium per slice, but it neglects interslice shear and does not satisfy complete force equilibrium. For circular slip surfaces in comparatively benign ground this is often acceptable, which is precisely why Bishop remains valuable as a rapid search method. It is not, however, a rigorous Spencer solution.

Spencer (1967) is the simplest broadly used method of slices that satisfies complete equilibrium of the discretised sliding mass under the assumption of parallel interslice resultants. As noted in the comparative reviews of Fredlund & Krahn (1977) and in later texts such as Duncan & Wright (2005), it is a rigorous method within the ordinary assumptions of limit-equilibrium slice analysis. In the present extension, Spencer shall therefore be implemented as a full and separate equilibrium solver: the same slice geometry, loads, pore pressures, and base lengths are reused, but the final Spencer factor of safety is obtained only from the Spencer equations themselves.

The key insight that makes Spencer practical is the observation that for a given slip surface:

  1. If you compute F from Spencer moment equilibrium as a function of λ, you obtain a curve F_m(λ).
  2. If you compute F from Spencer force equilibrium as a function of λ, you obtain a second curve F_f(λ).
  3. The true factor of safety is where these two curves intersect: F_m(λ*) = F_f(λ*) = F.

This is the principal conceptual addition over Bishop. Geometry, terrain, soil regions, slice generation, surcharge treatment, and pore pressure may all be reused. The solver itself, however, is not a Bishop solver with a Spencer overlay; it is a full Spencer equilibrium solve built on the same slice table.

Academic correction. The Spencer branch shall not reuse Bishop's governing width-form resistance terms or a fixed Bishop factor of safety as the Spencer moment branch. Bishop may be reused for search, initial bracketing, seeding, and QA comparison only. The final Spencer result shall come from the Spencer equations alone, using the true base length l_i = b_i / cos(α_i) and the corresponding c'_i l_i and u_i l_i terms on the base.

3Spencer Theory

3.1The interslice-force assumption

Spencer's method assumes that the resultant interslice force on every slice boundary is parallel to a single constant direction. Using the common component notation:

Spencer interslice-force assumption tan(θ) = X_i / E_i = λ for all interslice boundaries i where: E_i = horizontal component of the interslice resultant on boundary i X_i = vertical component of the interslice resultant on boundary i λ = tan(θ) θ = constant interslice-force inclination

This is the defining additional assumption that elevates the method from a Bishop-type approximation to a rigorous limit-equilibrium method of slices. It does not alter the geometry or load model; it alters the equilibrium kernel.

Implementation note. The code may use λ, θ, or a resultant-force notation internally. Any of these is acceptable, provided the algebra is consistent and the final solution satisfies both Spencer residual equations.

3.2Base force and strength terms

The Spencer branch uses the same slice geometry and loads as Bishop, but the shear-strength and pore-force terms are written on the true base length. This is the standard form given in Spencer (1967) and retained in later expositions such as Fredlund & Krahn (1977), Carpenter (1985), and Duncan & Wright (2005):

Base quantities used by Spencer l_i = b_i / cos(α_i) U_i = u_i l_i N'_i = N_i - U_i S_i = [c'_i l_i + N'_i tan(φ'_i)] / F where: b_i = horizontal slice width l_i = true base length u_i = representative pore pressure on the base U_i = base pore-force resultant N_i = total base normal resultant N'_i = effective base normal resultant S_i = mobilised base shear
Critical requirement. In the Spencer branch, the governing resistance shall be written on the true base length l_i, i.e. the cohesive and pore-force contributions shall enter the mobilised shear as c'_i l_i and u_i l_i. Width-form quantities such as c'_i b_i or u_i b_i may still appear in the downstream algebra, but only where they arise from a valid projection of the l_i-based terms — namely l_i cos α_i = b_i and l_i sin α_i = b_i tan α_i. A direct Bishop-style substitution of b_i for l_i in the governing strength equation is what is not academically correct.

3.3Two governing equations: F_m and F_f

For each assumed λ, the full Spencer formulation yields two factor-of-safety branches, both derived from the same slice equilibrium kernel:

Equation 1: Moment equilibrium → F_m(λ)

Moment-equilibrium branch R_m(F, λ) = 0 For fixed λ: find F_m(λ) such that the overall moment residual vanishes

For circular slips, the natural reference moment point is the circle centre. The implementation shall evaluate the Spencer slice forces and then compute the overall moment residual from those forces. Even when Spencer and Bishop give similar results for circular slips, the Spencer moment branch shall still be solved from the Spencer equations, not copied from Bishop.

Equation 2: Force equilibrium → F_f(λ)

Force-equilibrium branch R_f(F, λ) = 0 Equivalent implementation forms include: 1. the global horizontal force residual of the sliding mass 2. interslice-force closure E_n = 0, with E_0 = 0 For fixed λ: find F_f(λ) such that the chosen force residual vanishes

The closure form E_0 = 0, E_n = 0 is especially convenient in a left-to-right slice propagation routine and is widely used in software implementations.

3.4The intersection problem: solving for F and λ

The Spencer solution is the common root of the moment and force branches:

Spencer solution condition F_m(λ*) = F_f(λ*) = F* Equivalent residual form: R_m(F, λ) = 0 R_f(F, λ) = 0

Two implementation paths are academically valid. Both are found in the established literature and software lineage stemming from Spencer (1967), Morgenstern & Price (1965), Fredlund & Krahn (1977), and Carpenter (1985):

  1. Classical intersection method. For each λ, solve the moment and force branches separately to obtain F_m(λ) and F_f(λ), then solve g(λ) = F_m(λ) - F_f(λ) = 0.
  2. Direct two-variable solve. Solve the residual system R_m(F, λ) = 0, R_f(F, λ) = 0 simultaneously by Newton, secant, or quasi-Newton methods.

This specification adopts the classical intersection method for v2 because it is transparent, robust, and easy to benchmark. A direct two-variable solve remains acceptable if it is shown to reproduce the same Spencer result.

4Slice-Level Force Resolution — Full Derivation

4.1Common Spencer slice-state evaluator

Every Spencer branch evaluation shall call a common slice-state routine. That routine receives a trial (F, λ) together with the already prepared slice table and returns the slice forces and residuals for that trial pair.

Inputs and outputs of the Spencer slice kernel Inputs per slice: geometry → α_i, b_i, l_i, lever arms loads → W_i, Q_i, pore-pressure descriptor material → c'_i, φ'_i solver state → F, λ, E_left Outputs per slice: N_i, N'_i, S_i, E_right, X_right Outputs for the whole surface: momentResidual forceResidual max|E| slice-by-slice diagnostics

For each slice, three equilibrium conditions close the three unknowns N_i, S_i, and E_{i,right}:

Per-slice equilibrium equations Vertical: N_i cos α_i + S_i sin α_i = W_i + Q_i + (X_{i,left} - X_{i,right}) Horizontal: N_i sin α_i - S_i cos α_i = E_{i,right} - E_{i,left} Mohr-Coulomb: S_i = [c'_i l_i + (N_i - u_i l_i) tan φ'_i] / F Closure: X_{i,right} = λ E_{i,right} (Spencer assumption)

Eliminating S_i with the Mohr–Coulomb equation leaves a 2×2 linear system in (N_i, E_{i,right}) for fixed (F, λ, E_{i,left}). The resulting closed-form recurrence propagates the interslice force slice by slice.

Overall residuals Moment residual about the circle centre (circular surface): R_m(F, λ) = Σ_i W_i x_i - R Σ_i S_i Force residual (closure of the interslice chain): R_f(F, λ) = E_{n,right} with E_{0,left} = 0

The exact algebra may be coded in any form that is demonstrably equivalent to Spencer (1967) or Carpenter (1985): direct closed-form recurrence, linear approximation, or a small local Newton solve for the slice unknowns. The specification is concerned with the equilibrium target expressed above, not with enforcing one particular rearrangement, provided that the implemented equations remain traceable to the published Spencer formulation.

4.2Propagation and boundary conditions

For a chosen (F, λ), the slice chain is evaluated from one end of the slip surface to the other. In the convention adopted here, the propagation runs from the entry side to the exit side.

Boundary conditions and propagation E_0 = 0 X_i = λ E_i E_{i,right} becomes E_{i+1,left} At force-equilibrium convergence: E_n = 0

If the implementation instead evaluates a global horizontal-force residual directly, it shall still expose the equivalent closure residual for diagnostics and testing.

4.3Sign conventions and engineering checks

The sign convention shall be fixed once and used consistently in the solver, the tests, and the user-facing diagnostics:

  1. E_i shall be the signed horizontal component of the interslice force on boundary i.
  2. X_i shall always satisfy X_i = λE_i under the same sign convention.
  3. The reported N_eff shall mean the effective base normal force, not the total normal force. Stage 6 slice-table columns and Stage 7 report fields that previously carried a Bishop "normal force" shall be relabelled to effective normal force whenever a Spencer result is displayed, so that N_eff is never confused with the Bishop total-normal diagnostic.
  4. The Stage 6 table and Stage 7 report shall present the same sign convention used by the solver.
Engineering check. The interslice-force profile should usually be compressive over most of the sliding mass and taper toward the ends. Local tension near a boundary may occur, but widespread sign anomalies ordinarily indicate a sign-convention or equilibrium-kernel error.

5The Two-Equation Solver

5.1F from moment equilibrium (F_m)

For a fixed λ, the moment branch shall be solved from the Spencer residual, not from the Bishop factor of safety. The implementation shall bracket or seed the factor of safety and then solve:

Moment-branch solve Given λ: find F_m(λ) such that R_m(F, λ) = 0

Bishop is still useful here, but only as an auxiliary quantity: it may provide an initial guess, a bracket centre, and a comparison value stored in the result. It shall not be treated as the converged Spencer moment branch.

5.2F from force equilibrium (F_f)

For the same fixed λ, the force branch is solved from the Spencer force residual. The most practical residual for implementation is the closure of the interslice-force chain:

Force-branch solve Given λ: find F_f(λ) such that R_f(F, λ) = 0 Common implementation: E_0 = 0 propagate slice-by-slice for trial (F, λ) require E_n = 0 at convergence

The force branch is therefore another one-dimensional root-finding problem in F for fixed λ. Bisection is the v2 default because it is unconditionally convergent once the residual has opposite signs at the bracket endpoints; a safeguarded secant or Brent accelerator is permitted only after the bisection baseline has been benchmarked.

5.3Intersection by bisection (recommended for v2)

The recommended v2 strategy is the classical Spencer intersection method. The stopping criterion is the residual on g, not the width of the λ interval, so that tolerance scales with factor-of-safety units rather than with a dimensionless λ range:

Outer solver — bisection on λ g(λ) = F_m(λ) - F_f(λ) Choose an initial λ bracket, e.g.: λ_low = -0.6 λ_high = +0.6 Evaluate g(λ_low) and g(λ_high). Both must be valid and have opposite signs. If not, widen the bracket or mark the surface as Spencer-unresolved. Bisection loop: for iter = 1 to max_outer_iter: λ_mid = (λ_low + λ_high) / 2 Compute F_m(λ_mid) → solve R_m(F, λ_mid) = 0 Compute F_f(λ_mid) → solve R_f(F, λ_mid) = 0 g_mid = F_m(λ_mid) - F_f(λ_mid) if |g_mid| < intersection_tolerance: return converged at λ_mid if sign(g_mid) == sign(g(λ_low)): λ_low = λ_mid else: λ_high = λ_mid F_spencer = converged common value of F_m and F_f λ_spencer = λ_mid

The sign-compare update rule is used rather than a direct F_m > F_f test, because g(λ) is not guaranteed a priori to be monotone decreasing in λ for every surface. In practice g is monotone over a well-posed bracket, but the sign-compare form is robust to the exceptional cases without any extra cost.

At convergence, the reported Spencer factor of safety shall be the converged common value. The implementation shall not synthesize a Spencer factor by averaging an unconverged Bishop value and a force-equilibrium value.

5.4Direct two-variable solve (optional optimisation)

Once the intersection method is verified, the implementation may optionally replace it with a direct solve of the two Spencer residuals:

Direct solve in (F, λ) Solve simultaneously: R_m(F, λ) = 0 R_f(F, λ) = 0 Newton / quasi-Newton update: [F, λ]_{k+1} = [F, λ]_k - J^{-1} R where: R = [R_m, R_f]^T J = Jacobian of residuals with respect to [F, λ]

This route is faster once mature, but the classical branch-intersection method is the recommended baseline because it is easier to audit and benchmark against published Spencer workflows and against reference implementations derived from the same literature.

5.5Convergence, stability, and fallbacks

IssueSymptomRemedy
Moment residual cannot be bracketed No sign change in R_m(F, λ) for the chosen F bracket Widen the F bracket or mark this λ as unresolved for the moment branch.
Force residual cannot be bracketed No sign change in R_f(F, λ) for the chosen F bracket Widen the F bracket. If still unresolved, move to the next λ or mark the surface Spencer-unresolved.
g(λ) does not change sign across the λ bracket The two Spencer branches do not intersect in the initial λ range Widen the λ range. If the branches still do not intersect, flag the surface and retain the Bishop comparison result for the UI.
N'_i becomes negative for some slice Tension develops on the base Flag it. Whether tension is clipped to zero or merely reported shall be an explicit implementation choice and shall be benchmarked, not introduced silently.
Interslice forces E become negative Tension develops between slices Flag for review. Mild local tension near a boundary may occur; strong or widespread tension usually warrants scrutiny of the surface and solver state.
Robust fallback strategy. In the user-facing application, a failed Spencer solve may fall back to the Bishop result so that the engineer is not blocked. In the Spencer result object itself, however, the implementation shall not report a fabricated Spencer factor of safety when the Spencer residuals have not converged.

7Complete Pseudocode

7.1Common Spencer state evaluator New

// ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
// evaluateSpencerState: common Spencer slice kernel
//
// Inputs:
//   slices  → geometry, loads, pore pressure, material data
//   F       → trial factor of safety
//   lambda  → trial interslice-force ratio
//
// Outputs:
//   sliceStates     → N_total, N_eff, S_mob, E_right, X_right
//   momentResidual  → overall Spencer moment residual
//   forceResidual   → overall Spencer force residual / closure residual
//
// The slice equations shall be transcribed from Spencer (1967) or
// Carpenter (1985), using l_i on cohesion and pore-force terms.
// ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
function evaluateSpencerState(
  slices: Slice[],
  lambda: float,
  F: float
) → {
  valid: bool,
  sliceStates: {N_total, N_eff, S_mob, E_right, X_right}[],
  momentResidual: float,
  forceResidual: float
}:

  let E_left = 0.0
  let states = []
  let momentResidual = 0.0

  for each s in slices:
    // Solve the Spencer slice equations for this slice.
    // Governing ingredients:
    //   l_i  = b_i / cos(alpha_i)
    //   U_i  = u_i l_i
    //   S_i  = [c'_i l_i + N'_i tan(phi'_i)] / F
    //   X_i  = lambda E_i
    // The implementation may use a direct recurrence, Carpenter's
    // linear approximation method, or an algebraically equivalent
    // local solve, provided the resulting residuals are Spencer-true.

    let state = solveSliceSpencerEq(s, F, lambda, E_left)
    if not state.valid:
      return {valid: false, sliceStates: states, momentResidual: NaN, forceResidual: NaN}

    states.push(state)
    momentResidual += computeSliceMomentAboutCenter(s, state)
    E_left = state.E_right

  let forceResidual = E_left
  return {valid: true, sliceStates: states, momentResidual, forceResidual}

7.2Moment branch solve New

// ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
// computeFm: solve R_m(F, lambda) = 0 for fixed lambda
// ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
function computeFm(
  slices: Slice[],
  lambda: float,
  config: SpencerConfig
) → {F_m: float, converged: bool, state}:

  let F_lo = config.F_bracket_low
  let F_hi = config.F_bracket_high
  let res_lo = evaluateSpencerState(slices, lambda, F_lo)
  let res_hi = evaluateSpencerState(slices, lambda, F_hi)

  if not res_lo.valid or not res_hi.valid:
    return {F_m: NaN, converged: false, state: null}
  if sign(res_lo.momentResidual) == sign(res_hi.momentResidual):
    return {F_m: NaN, converged: false, state: null}

  for iter = 1 to config.max_inner_iter:
    let F_mid = (F_lo + F_hi) / 2.0
    let res_mid = evaluateSpencerState(slices, lambda, F_mid)
    if not res_mid.valid:
      return {F_m: NaN, converged: false, state: null}
    if abs(res_mid.momentResidual) < config.moment_tolerance:
      return {F_m: F_mid, converged: true, state: res_mid}
    if sign(res_mid.momentResidual) == sign(res_lo.momentResidual):
      F_lo = F_mid; res_lo = res_mid
    else:
      F_hi = F_mid; res_hi = res_mid

  return {F_m: (F_lo + F_hi) / 2.0, converged: false, state: res_hi}

Checks on the common state evaluator 1. E_0 = 0 by boundary condition 2. E_n = 0 at force-branch convergence 3. X_i = λ E_i for every boundary 4. N_eff, S_mob, E_right, X_right are finite for every slice 5. The reported residuals are consistent with the stored slice states

7.4Outer solver: find λ where F_m = F_f New

// ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
// computeSpencerF: complete Spencer solution by branch intersection
//
// Bishop may be computed as a seed or comparison value only.
// ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
function computeSpencerF(
  slices: Slice[],
  config: SpencerConfig
) → StabilityResult:

  let bishopResult = computeBishopF(slices)   // optional seed and comparison

  function g(lambda: float) → {valid: bool, value: float, F_m: float, F_f: float}:
    let fm = computeFm(slices, lambda, config)
    let ff = computeFf(slices, lambda, config)
    if not fm.converged or not ff.converged:
      return {valid: false, value: NaN, F_m: NaN, F_f: NaN}
    return {
      valid: true,
      value: fm.F_m - ff.F_f,
      F_m: fm.F_m,
      F_f: ff.F_f
    }

  let lambdaLo = config.lambda_low
  let lambdaHi = config.lambda_high
  let gLo = g(lambdaLo)
  let gHi = g(lambdaHi)

  if not gLo.valid or not gHi.valid:
    return unresolvedSpencerResult(bishopResult, "Spencer branch solve failed at λ bracket endpoints")
  if sign(gLo.value) == sign(gHi.value):
    return unresolvedSpencerResult(bishopResult, "F_m and F_f do not intersect in the current λ range")

  for iter = 1 to config.max_outer_iter:
    let lambdaMid = (lambdaLo + lambdaHi) / 2.0
    let gMid = g(lambdaMid)
    if not gMid.valid:
      return unresolvedSpencerResult(bishopResult, "Spencer branch solve failed during λ bisection")

    if abs(gMid.value) < config.intersection_tolerance:
      // Re-evaluate the kernel once at the converged (λ*, F*) to produce
      // a single self-consistent slice-state set for diagnostics.
      let F_star = 0.5 * (gMid.F_m + gMid.F_f)
      let finalState = evaluateSpencerState(slices, lambdaMid, F_star)
      populateSliceDiagnosticsFromState(slices, finalState, lambdaMid)
      return StabilityResult {
        method: SPENCER,
        F: F_star,
        converged: true,
        iterations: iter,
        slices: slices,
        lambda: lambdaMid,
        theta_deg: degrees(atan(lambdaMid)),
        F_m: gMid.F_m,
        F_f: gMid.F_f,
        F_bishop: bishopResult?.F ?? NaN,
        momentResidual: finalState.momentResidual,
        forceResidual: finalState.forceResidual,
        maxE: max(abs(st.E_right) for st in finalState.sliceStates),
        tensionSlices: count(st.N_eff < 0 for st in finalState.sliceStates),
        rejected: false,
        rejectReason: ""
      }

    if sign(gMid.value) == sign(gLo.value):
      lambdaLo = lambdaMid
      gLo = gMid
    else:
      lambdaHi = lambdaMid
      gHi = gMid

  return unresolvedSpencerResult(bishopResult, "Outer λ loop did not converge")

7.5Integrated search driver Modify

// ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
// searchWithSpencer: search using Bishop, re-check top-N with Spencer
//
// This is the recommended integration strategy:
// 1. Run the full search with Bishop (fast)
// 2. Take the top-N critical surfaces
// 3. Re-analyse each with Spencer (slower but rigorous)
// 4. Re-rank by Spencer F
// ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
function searchWithSpencer(
  model: SlopeModel,
  searchConfig: SearchConfig,
  spencerConfig: SpencerConfig,
  recheckCount: int = 10
) → SearchResult:

  // ── Phase 1: Bishop search (unchanged from v1) ──
  let bishopSearch = searchGridRadius(model, searchConfig)
    // This returns results sorted by Bishop F, with slices pre-built

  // ── Phase 2: full Spencer re-check on top candidates ──
  let spencerResults = []

  for each bishopResult in bishopSearch.results.take(recheckCount):
    // Reuse the slices, but solve Spencer from its own equations.
    // Carry over the Bishop-side comparison values (F and, if available, F_fellenius)
    // as secondary fields on the Spencer result — never as the Spencer F itself.
    let spencerResult = computeSpencerF(bishopResult.slices, spencerConfig)
    spencerResult.circle = bishopResult.circle
    spencerResult.F_bishop = bishopResult.F
    if bishopResult.F_fellenius != null:
      spencerResult.F_fellenius = bishopResult.F_fellenius
    spencerResults.push(spencerResult)

  // ── Phase 3: rank by Spencer F ──
  let convergedSpencer = spencerResults.filter(r → r.converged)
  convergedSpencer.sortBy(r → r.F)

  // If no Spencer result converged, fall back to Bishop ranking
  if convergedSpencer.length == 0:
    return bishopSearch   // unchanged Bishop results

  return SearchResult {
    results: convergedSpencer,
    critical: convergedSpencer[0],
    trialsTotal: bishopSearch.trialsTotal,
    trialsValid: bishopSearch.trialsValid,
    trialsRejected: bishopSearch.trialsRejected,
    // Additional Spencer-specific metadata:
    spencerRechecked: recheckCount,
    spencerConverged: convergedSpencer.length
  }
Search strategy recap. Bishop-for-search and Spencer-for-shortlist remains the recommended runtime architecture. That is an optimisation strategy only; it does not change the mathematical requirement that the shortlisted verification be a full Spencer solve.

8Validation Strategy

Cross-check against Bishop

For circular slips in comparatively uniform soil, Spencer and Bishop will often lie close. That is a useful engineering check, but it is not the primary validation criterion. The primary validation criterion is that the Spencer residuals themselves converge correctly and match benchmark solutions.

Expected Bishop–Spencer agreement For circular surfaces in uniform soil: |F_spencer - F_bishop| / F_bishop < 5% (usually < 2%) If the difference exceeds 5%, check: - Is the exit angle steep? (Bishop degrades for steep exits) - Is there a large strength contrast between layers? - Is pore pressure high? (u/γh > 0.4) Large differences are a prompt for review, not proof of error.

Interslice force validation

After a Spencer solution, the interslice forces provide rich diagnostic information:

  1. E profile shape: should be roughly parabolic — zero at both ends, positive (compressive) in the middle. Plot E vs. slice index.
  2. X = λ·E: verify this holds exactly for every slice boundary.
  3. No large tension: if N'_i is negative for many slices (not just 1–2 near the crest), the slip surface geometry is suspect.
  4. λ magnitude: typical converged λ is between 0 and 0.5 for most slopes. Values outside [-0.5, +0.8] are unusual and worth investigating.
  5. Global force balance: sum of all horizontal forces should be zero to within tolerance. This is the strongest check — if it fails, the solver has a bug.

Benchmark test cases

Use the same geometric test cases from the v1 spec (§10), but add the following Spencer-specific acceptance checks:

TestAcceptance criterionExpected Spencer behaviourExpected λ range
Test 1: Homogeneous, dry Residuals converge; benchmark close to reference software Spencer close to Bishop; force profile smooth and compressive 0.05 – 0.25
Test 2: Homogeneous, water table Residuals converge; benchmark close to reference software Sensitivity to pore pressure reproduced consistently 0.10 – 0.30
Test 3: Two-layer, dry Residuals converge; benchmark close to reference software Difference from Bishop may be more pronounced than Test 1 0.05 – 0.20

Additionally, verify against an established reference implementation such as STABL, SLOPE/W, Slide, or an independently checked spreadsheet. Compare not only F, but also λ, the residual magnitudes, and the interslice-force profile.

9Practical Integration Notes

UI implications

The Spencer extension adds the following to the user interface:

The Spencer toggle

The primary UI control is a single checkbox or toggle switch labelled "Spencer equilibrium check" (default: ON). Its behaviour is simple and deterministic:

Toggle behaviour Spencer toggle OFF: → Search uses Bishop only → Results show: F_bishop → Solver call: searchGridRadius() → computeBishopF() → Fastest mode — suitable for exploration and initial geometry tuning Spencer toggle ON: → Search uses Bishop, then re-checks top-N with Spencer → Results show: F_spencer (primary), F_bishop (secondary, for comparison) → Solver call: searchWithSpencer() → computeBishopF() + computeSpencerF() → If Spencer fails to converge on a surface, Bishop result is shown with a note "Spencer did not converge" → Slower than Bishop-only, but still practical because only the shortlist is re-checked Implementation: The toggle sets a single boolean in the search config: config.useSpencer: bool = true The search driver checks this flag: if config.useSpencer: return searchWithSpencer(model, searchConfig, spencerConfig) else: return searchGridRadius(model, searchConfig)

Additional display elements when Spencer is enabled:

  1. Method selector: let the user choose "Bishop only" (fast, for exploration) or "Bishop + Spencer check" (rigorous, for final results). Default to Bishop + Spencer.
  2. Result display: show both F_bishop and F_spencer for the critical surface. If they agree within 2%, display only one (with a note "confirmed by Spencer"). If they differ by more than 2%, show both prominently.
  3. Interslice force diagram: optionally plot the E and X distributions along the slip surface. This is a standard output in commercial tools and gives engineers confidence that the result is physically plausible.
  4. λ / θ display: show the converged interslice-force angle. This is informational — the user never sets it.
  5. Convergence indicator: if Spencer failed to converge for a surface, show the Bishop result with a note "Spencer did not converge — Bishop comparison shown".

Performance budget

PhaseSurfacesCost per surfaceTotal
Coarse Bishop search ~3,400 ~150 slice-loop evaluations ~510k evals → <1 s
Refined Bishop search ~3,500 ~150 slice-loop evaluations ~525k evals → <1 s
Spencer re-check 10 ~300–800 kernel calls (outer λ × inner F bisection) ~3k–8k kernel calls → <0.1 s
Total ~1.04M evals → ~2 s

These figures are indicative only. The Spencer cost per surface is set by the product of the outer λ-bisection count and the two inner F-bisection counts; with the default max_outer_iter = 20 and max_inner_iter = 30 the worst case is roughly 20×(30+30) = 1200 kernel calls, and typical convergence is well under half of that. Because the Spencer cost is paid on only the shortlisted surfaces, the overall runtime remains dominated by the Bishop search.

Error handling hierarchy

  1. If geometry/slicing fails → reject the surface (same as v1).
  2. If Bishop fails to converge → reject the surface (same as v1).
  3. If Bishop converges but Spencer fails → return the Bishop result, flagged as "Spencer unconverged". Never discard a valid Bishop result because Spencer had trouble.
  4. If Spencer converges → return the Spencer result (with Bishop F stored for comparison).

10Onward to Morgenstern–Price

With Spencer implemented, the step to Morgenstern–Price is small. The only change is that the interslice-force ratio is no longer constant:

Morgenstern–Price interslice-force function Spencer: X_i / E_i = λ (constant) M-P: X_i / E_i = λ · f(x_i) (variable) where f(x) is a prescribed function normalised so that max(f) = 1. Common choices: f(x) = 1 → reduces to Spencer f(x) = sin(π·ξ) → half-sine (most common default in SLOPE/W) f(x) = trapezoid → user-defined linear segments ξ = (x - x_entry) / (x_exit - x_entry) (normalised position)

The implementation changes are:

  1. Replace the constant side-force ratio λ in the common Spencer state evaluator with λ · f(x_i).
  2. Add a function f(x) to the config (default: half-sine).
  3. The outer solver remains a scalar solve in λ for the prescribed side-force function.

For circular slip surfaces, M-P with half-sine f(x) typically gives F within 0.5% of Spencer. The method becomes more valuable when you move to non-circular surfaces (composite, block, or optimised shapes), where the interslice-force distribution matters more and the constant-ratio assumption of Spencer becomes less physical.

Recommended sequence. Once Spencer is verified and stable, adding M-P is a half-day task. The bigger investment at that point is non-circular surface search (block search, optimisation, or Cuckoo search), which is a geometry problem rather than a solver problem. Your current architecture (closed polygonal regions, polygon-clipping slice builder) already supports non-circular surfaces — you would only need to replace the circle-based yBase calculation with a general polyline-based one.
